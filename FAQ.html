Private mortgage education

General information only — not financial advice. Terms vary by lender and situation; speak with a licensed mortgage professional about your specific circumstances.

What's the difference between a private mortgage and a bank mortgage?

Private mortgages are funded by private lenders or lending networks rather than a bank or credit union. They are often used when a borrower doesn't fit traditional bank criteria around income documentation, credit history, or property type, and can close faster — though rates, fees, and terms vary by lender and deal.

What does loan-to-value (LTV) mean?

LTV compares the mortgage amount to the appraised value of the property, expressed as a percentage. Lenders use LTV to assess risk, and the maximum LTV a lender will approve depends on the lender, the property type, and the borrower's overall profile.

What costs come with a private mortgage?

Beyond the interest rate, private mortgages typically involve lender fees, broker fees, legal fees, and sometimes an appraisal fee. These vary by lender and deal, which is why it's important to review a full cost breakdown before accepting an offer.

What are typical private mortgage terms?

Private mortgages are usually structured as short-term, often interest-only financing rather than the long amortization periods common with bank mortgages. They're frequently used as a bridge while a borrower improves their financial position or arranges longer-term financing.

What risks should borrowers understand?

Private mortgages typically carry higher interest rates and fees than bank financing, and shorter terms mean borrowers need a clear plan for renewal, refinancing, or sale before the term ends. Review all fees, the renewal process, and your exit strategy before signing.

What is an exit strategy, and why does it matter?

An exit strategy is the borrower's plan for paying off or refinancing a private mortgage before its term ends — for example, selling the property, qualifying for bank financing, or improving credit. Lenders typically want to understand this plan as part of approval.
